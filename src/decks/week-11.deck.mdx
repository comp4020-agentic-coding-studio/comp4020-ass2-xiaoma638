# Three people is not a study

SLOP2805 · Week 11 · Lecture

Somebody who has never seen your interface is about to use it,

hit a stall you did not warn them about,

and say out loud what they think is happening.

---

## Where we were, and where we are going

**Week 10** produced a register of every claim your interface makes, all of them
now traced to an event.

**Today** is the only evidence in the course that comes from outside your own
head — and it is much weaker than you will want it to be.

---

## Existence proofs and rates

> **"At least one person read the stall as a failure."**
> Established by **one** observation. Real, reportable, often enough to act on.

> **"Most people read the stall as a failure."**
> Not established by three observations, or thirty, or any number you will reach.

Nearly every overreach in a write-up is the first sentence rewritten as the
second.

**Do not write proportions.** "Two of three" counts this room. It is not a rate.

---

## Records, not readings

> ✗ **"The user was confused."**
> A conclusion with its evidence deleted.

> ✓ **"They moved the pointer to Cancel, stopped, and said *is it stuck or is it
> me*."**
> A record.

Only the second survives next week, when you disagree with yourself about what
it meant.

---

## Name your sample precisely

Not "a small sample". **These** people:

- they are in this course
- they know a stall is coming, because stalls were week 8
- they have built the same interface
- they are simultaneously the most forgiving and the most suspicious users your
  prototype will ever have

Generic humility is not a limitation statement. Specific bias is.

---

## Worked example — one observation, three legitimate answers

**What was recorded (P2, simulated stall at 45%):**

> *pointer to Cancel at 0:38 · did not click · "is it frozen or is it just
> slow" · pressed Cancel at 0:52*

| Answer | When it is right |
| --- | --- |
| **Change it.** Show elapsed-since-last-progress after 10 s | you saw it twice, you understand the cause, the fix is smaller than the problem |
| **Leave it, say why.** One person, first use, cannot separate novelty | you saw it once |
| **Leave it, name the test.** "Show 5 people a 60 s stall; count who cancels before 45 s" | you think it is real and one observation cannot settle it |

All three score. **Only the unexamined change loses marks.**

## Your turn — Thursday

1. **Rotation**, groups of four. Three rounds of 15 minutes each way.
2. **Consent read aloud** before each round; stop if asked.
3. **Fill your sheets before discussing anything with anyone.**
4. **Three handling decisions**, each naming the observation behind it.
5. **At most three changes.** A ceiling, not a target.

**Not compulsory** — in either role, for any reason, with no explanation. Tell a
tutor and you get the structured walkthrough, graded identically.

## The mistake to expect

Manufacturing a change to look responsive.

> Three changes on one thin observation apiece is a **worse** outcome than one
> change and two honest deferrals, and it is marked as one.

The other mistake belongs to the alternative task: a **walkthrough written up as
though somebody had been watched**. A walkthrough is evidence about internal
consistency, not about people. That is the one way to lose this criterion
outright.

---

## What this week cannot tell you

How often anything happens. Whether your interface is good. Whether a stranger
under real pressure, with a file they needed, would do what P2 did.

You are marked on whether your conclusions are **proportionate to your
evidence** — never on how many people you watched, whether you watched anybody,
or how many things you changed.

**A confident claim from three observations loses marks. A careful claim from one
does not.**

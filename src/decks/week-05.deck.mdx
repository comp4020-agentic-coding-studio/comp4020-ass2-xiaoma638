# Why the estimate keeps jumping

SLOP2805 · Week 5

**"About 4 minutes remaining" is not a measurement. It is a prediction** — and
predictions have error. Today is not about removing that error. It is about what
you are allowed to put on the screen once you know how big it is.

---

## The case

`trace-office-wifi.json` — a real-shaped upload, recorded once and replayed
identically for everybody in this room.

- 60 MB
- finished in **94 seconds**
- fast for 40 s, then **12 seconds of almost nothing**, then recovery

Everything today runs over this one file, so when your number differs from your
neighbour's, the estimator is the only thing that differs.

**Simulated data.** The trace is recorded sample data shipped with the course
prototype, not a live capture taken during class. That is what makes it
replayable, and replayable is the whole reason the exercise works.

---

## What the user saw

A naive estimate, refreshed every two seconds, across the stall:

| t | displayed |
| --- | --- |
| 36 s | 41 s remaining |
| 40 s | 39 s remaining |
| 44 s | **2 min 10 s** |
| 48 s | **6 min 40 s** |
| 52 s | **11 min** |
| 56 s | 3 min 20 s |
| 60 s | 52 s |

Every one of those was **correctly computed**.

---

## One equation, three readings of one word

```
remaining work  ÷  rate
```

The work is settled — you chose a denominator in
[week 3](/sessions/03-where-is-the-denominator/).

So the entire design space is: **what does "rate" mean?**

---

## 1 · Naive linear

`rate = progress / elapsed`

Assumes the future looks like **the whole past**.

- very stable while conditions hold
- early fast seconds keep propping the average up
- so it is wrong for a long time *after* things change — and wrong in the
  reassuring direction

---

## 2 · Sliding window

`rate = Δprogress over the last n seconds`

Assumes the future looks like **the recent past**.

- reacts to the stall quickly — that is the point
- reacts to every ordinary fluctuation — that is the cost
- **the window length is the whole design**: too short is noise, too long is
  the naive estimator wearing a hat

---

## 3 · Exponentially weighted

`rate = α · sample + (1 − α) · rate`

Assumes the future looks like the recent past, **smoothly**.

- one parameter, no buffer
- gentler response to a spike
- α is doing the same job the window length did, and is just as much of a
  design decision

---

## None of them knows about the stall — and they should not

An estimator turns a rate into a time.

Noticing that the process has **stopped** is a different job, from a different
signal — the timestamp of the last progress event — and it belongs to
[week 8](/sessions/08-stuck-timed-out-disconnected/).

Two clean mechanisms beat one clever one. Keep them apart.

---

## Measuring instability

Same measure for all three, or you cannot compare them:

> **mean absolute change in the displayed value between consecutive refreshes**,
> in seconds

Note what this does **not** measure: how close the estimate got to 94 seconds.

It measures what the user experiences — how much the number jumps.

---

## The counterexample

Run the same three over `trace-steady.json`: 60 MB, no stall, even rate.

The ranking **reverses**. Sliding window and exponential smoothing now spend
their reactivity on noise that means nothing, while naive linear — the estimator
that failed so badly ten slides ago — is the calmest and the most accurate.

**There is no best estimator. There is a best estimator for a traffic pattern,
and you do not get to know the pattern in advance.**

## Discuss — 25 minutes

> A smooth estimate that is wrong for twenty seconds,
> or a correct estimate that visibly panics.
>
> **Which do you ship, and who does your answer disadvantage?**

Consider: someone deciding whether to leave their desk. Someone on a metered
connection. Someone who has already lost this file once.

## What this lecture carries

**Existence proofs and rates.** The single most useful distinction in small-n
observation:

- *"At least one person read the stall as a failure."* Established by one
  observation. Real, reportable, and often enough to act on.
- *"Most people read the stall as a failure."* Not established by three
  observations, or by thirty, or by any number you will reach this semester.

Nearly every overreach in a student write-up — and in a good deal of published
design writing — is the first sentence rewritten as the second. Watch for
proportions: "two of three" is a count of who was in the room, not a rate.

**Recording behaviour, not readings.** "The user was confused" is a conclusion
with its evidence deleted. "They moved the pointer to Cancel, stopped, and said
'is it stuck or is it me'" is a record. Only the second can be re-examined next
week when you disagree with yourself about what it meant.

**Naming your sample precisely.** Your participants are classmates. They know a
stall is coming, because stalls were week 8. They have built this interface
themselves. The useful move is not to apologise for this in general terms but to
say what it specifically means: they will spot your indeterminate state faster
than any real user, and they will be far more patient with it.

**Deciding not to change something.** Three legitimate responses to an
observation: change it, leave it and say why, or leave it and name the test that
would settle it. Manufacturing a change to demonstrate responsiveness is the
failure mode this lecture exists to prevent. On
[Assignment 3](/assessments/honest-waiting-kit/) you are marked on whether your
conclusions are proportionate to your evidence, never on how many people you
watched or how many things you changed.

**The alternative task.** The structured walkthrough available in
[week 11's studio](/sessions/11-watching-someone-wait/) is evidence about an
interface's internal consistency. It is not evidence about people. It is graded
identically and it must never be written up as though somebody had been watched.

## A worked example

One observation, written two ways. Only one of them is supportable.

> A participant reaches for Cancel during the stall, stops, and says *"is it
> frozen or is it just slow"*.

**Supportable:** *at least one person could not tell a stall from slow progress.*
That is an existence proof. One observation establishes it, it is reportable, and
it is often enough to act on.

**Not supportable:** *users cannot tell a stall from slow progress.* No number of
classmates gets you there. Nor does *two of three*, which is a count of who was
in the room.

The first sentence licenses a change. The second licenses a claim, and you do not
have one.

## What this lecture cannot settle

This lecture tells you what you cannot conclude. It cannot tell you what is
worth watching for.

That comes from the claims register you built in week 10: the rows you marked
`kept with reason` are the ones where your own judgement was doing the work, and
they are where an outside reaction is most informative. Arriving on Thursday
without having decided what to look at produces three pleasant sessions and no
findings.

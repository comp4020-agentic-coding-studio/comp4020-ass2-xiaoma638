Three pieces of work, no examination, and nothing submitted twice.

## They accumulate

The assessments are not three separate tasks that happen to be in the same
course. Each is the same analytic move applied to a harder object, and each one
is built out of something the previous one produced.

| Assessment | What it asks | Built from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Progress Bar Autopsy** | analyse a waiting interface **you did not build** | nothing — this is where the method is learned |
| **Waiting Interface Prototype** | apply that analysis to **your own** interface, working | the autopsy's claim-and-source method, and the state model it made you infer |
| **Honest Waiting Kit** | make that interface survive **failure**, and account for every claim left on it | the prototype, its audit sheet's failures, and the studio deliverables from weeks 10 and 11 |

Each brief says explicitly what it hands on and what it inherits. The criteria do
not overlap: *Behaviour across the four conditions* is marked in Assignment 2 and
never again; *Behaviour under abnormal conditions* is marked in Assignment 3 and
not before it. A prototype that does not change between the two scores on
neither.

## What that means for feedback

Because nothing is resubmitted, feedback has somewhere to go. A correction to
your reasoning in the autopsy lands on your own screen four weeks later, where
the same reasoning is marked on something you built. A line your marker breaks
on the Assignment 2 audit sheet is the first row of your Assignment 3 claims
register.

## How marks are earned above "it works"

Assignment 3 carries half the course, and its brief sets out the difference
between **meeting the requirement** and **arguing it well**, criterion by
criterion. The short version: the requirement is about the artefact, the
argument is about the decision behind it.
